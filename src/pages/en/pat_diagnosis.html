What low vision is

You have low vision when you see poorly and glasses, contact lenses, medicines or surgery cannot fully fix it. This makes some everyday things harder: reading, recognising faces, cooking, getting around outside or using your phone.

Low vision does not mean blindness. Most people keep some sight, known as residual vision. With the right aids and training, it can be used much better.

When sight is very reduced, people talk about legal blindness, even if the person still sees a little. This website is for you too if you are blind: it works with screen readers and braille displays.

First steps

  1. Understand your diagnosis

    Ask your ophthalmologist what condition you have, whether there is treatment, how it may progress and how often it should be checked. You can ask them to write it down or bring someone with you.

  2. Ask for a low vision assessment

    An optometrist specialising in low vision measures how you see in real situations and suggests aids for what you want to do: reading, cooking, watching TV, walking outside… Where to ask for one.

  3. Learn to use the aids

    Vision rehabilitation teaches you techniques (for example, looking “to the side” if you cannot see the centre) and supports you until you feel comfortable.

  4. Connect with others

    Associations and peer support groups put you in touch with people who have been through the same.

  5. Look after how you feel

    There is no need to wait until you feel very low before asking for psychological support.

Words you will hear

A basic glossary. See the full glossary for more terms.

Visual acuity
How well you see fine detail. It is measured with letters that get smaller.
Visual field
All the space you can see without moving your eyes, including the sides.
Residual vision
The sight you still have and can make use of.
Selective filters
Tinted lenses that reduce glare and improve contrast.
Assistive technology
Technology for people with sight loss: screen readers, braille displays, electronic magnifiers…
Vision rehabilitation
Training to make better use of your sight and carry out daily activities more independently.

Myths and facts

Common misconceptions about low vision
People say…The facts
“Using my eyes a lot will wear them out.”No. Reading close up, with a magnifier or close to the screen does not make the condition worse. If you get tired, rest.
“Nothing more can be done.”Even when a condition has no cure, aids, rehabilitation, technology and support make daily life much better.
“Anyone with a white cane is totally blind.”No. Many people with low vision see partly and use a cane only at night, in unfamiliar places or in poor light.
“If she can read her phone, she sees fine.”You can have good central vision and a very narrow field at the same time, so you may trip or walk into doors.
“With a positive attitude you can do anything.”Not quite. Some things will be harder or need doing differently. Saying so is not being negative.
“Aids are for older people.”Aids and technology are useful at any age, including for study and work.

What can and cannot be done

An honest guide, without discouragement or false promises.

{{icon:check}} You can

  • Make better use of your sight with optical and electronic aids and filters.
  • Keep reading, with a magnifier, large print, your phone or audio.
  • Adapt your home to make it safer and more comfortable.
  • Use your phone and computer with magnification or a screen reader.
  • Get around more safely with orientation and mobility training.
  • Keep studying, working and doing activities, with adjustments.

{{icon:info}} It helps to know that

  • Ordinary glasses cannot repair a damaged retina or optic nerve.
  • Many conditions have no cure; treatment often slows them down.
  • Some activities, such as driving, may no longer be possible if legal requirements are not met.
  • Aids take time to learn: they can be tiring at first.
  • Be wary of “miracle” treatments sold online.

How you feel

Losing sight can bring sadness, fear, anger or exhaustion. This is a normal reaction. Talking helps: with family, with the professionals who care for you or with others going through the same.

If you feel sad most days or do not feel like doing anything, ask for help. There are psychologists who specialise in sight loss.

Some people with significant sight loss see things that are not there (shapes, patterns, people). This is called Charles Bonnet syndrome. It does not mean you have a mental health problem. Tell your eye care professional.

{{icon:alert}}

Important: when to go to A&E

Go to an emergency department straight away (or call 112) if you notice:

  • sudden loss of vision, in one or both eyes;
  • straight lines that suddenly look bent or wavy;
  • flashes of light, many new floaters or a curtain-like shadow;
  • severe eye pain with a red eye, especially with headache or nausea.

If you have any of these signs and are given an appointment months away, insist: it is an emergency.

Frequently asked questions

Will I go blind?

Most people with low vision do not lose all their sight. It depends on the cause and on each person. Ask your ophthalmologist how your condition may progress.

Why don't ordinary glasses help enough?

Glasses correct focusing problems, but they cannot repair a damaged retina or optic nerve. Low vision aids work differently: they make things bigger, increase contrast or reduce glare.

Can I keep driving?

Driving requires minimum vision standards. Your eye care professional can tell you whether you meet them. Do not drive if in doubt.

Are aids expensive?

Prices vary widely. Many tools are already free on your phone. Some organisations and public bodies offer grants for assistive products. Always try them before buying.
