Optical aids, technology and apps

No single aid works for everyone. The best one suits your vision and what you want to do. Often several are combined.

Before you choose

  • Try before you buy. A low vision professional will let you try several aids with your real tasks.
  • Think about the goal. Reading letters is not the same as seeing the bus number or cooking.
  • Allow time to learn. A magnifier can be tiring at first; with practice it gets easier.
  • Review over time. If your sight changes, you may need a different aid.

Optical aids

  • {{icon:search}} Magnifiers

    Hand-held, stand or illuminated. For reading, labels or crafts. The stronger the magnification, the closer you hold it and the less text you see at once.

  • {{icon:eye}} Special glasses

    High-powered reading glasses (high addition) or prism glasses. They keep your hands free.

  • {{icon:target}} Telescopes

    Small hand-held or spectacle-mounted telescopes for distance: signs, the board, bus numbers.

Selective filters

These are tinted lenses (yellow, orange, red or brown) that block part of the light, especially blue light. They reduce glare, improve contrast and help you adjust to changes in light when entering or leaving a building.

  • There are different colours and strengths: they should be tried indoors and outdoors.
  • Very useful for photophobia, retinitis pigmentosa, cataracts, corneal problems or after some surgery.
  • They are not ordinary sunglasses: a low vision professional helps you choose.

Electronic aids

  • {{icon:text}} Electronic magnifiers

    A camera and screen that enlarge text and let you change colours (for example, white or yellow text on black). Portable and desktop models exist.

  • {{icon:speaker}} Text-to-speech readers

    Devices or apps that photograph text and read it aloud: letters, recipes, labels.

  • {{icon:book}} Books and audio

    Audiobooks, podcasts, large-print books and smart speakers to check the time or weather or make calls by voice.

Screen readers and braille

Assistive technology lets you use phones and computers with little or no sight:

  • Screen magnification and large text, built into phones and computers.
  • Screen readers, which read everything on screen aloud: VoiceOver (iPhone, iPad and Mac), TalkBack (Android), NVDA (Windows, free) and JAWS.
  • Braille displays: a device that shows on-screen text in braille.
  • If you use a public computer without these programs, this website has a Listen to this page button in the top bar.

ONCE and associations offer courses, and some libraries have adapted computers.

Research advances

Research is moving forward, and it is natural to feel hopeful. It is also important to know where things stand:

  • Gene therapy: there is already an approved treatment for one retinal dystrophy caused by a specific gene, and many others are in trials. A genetic test is needed to know whether it could apply to you.
  • AMD treatments: injections for wet AMD have been used for years; for dry AMD, new medicines are being studied and are approved in some countries.
  • Leber optic neuropathy: there is a medicine approved in Europe and gene therapies under study.
  • Retinal implants, stem cells and optogenetics: still experimental.

Ask your ophthalmologist whether there are trials for your condition. You can search registered trials at ClinicalTrials.gov. Be wary of clinics charging for unapproved “miracle” treatments.
