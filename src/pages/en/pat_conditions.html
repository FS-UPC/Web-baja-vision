Understanding my condition

You can look up your condition or what you notice: whether you cannot see the centre, the sides, or whether light bothers you. Understanding what is happening helps you explain it and choose aids.

By type of sight loss

The same condition can affect people differently. Knowing your type of sight loss matters as much as the name of the condition. You can see what these losses look like in the vision simulator.

{{icon:target}} I cannot see the centre well

Reading, recognising faces or seeing a screen is hard, but you can walk without tripping. Typical of AMD, Stargardt disease, macular holes and some optic nerve conditions.

Helps: magnification (magnifiers, phone), more light and learning to look “to the side” (eccentric viewing).

{{icon:eye}} I cannot see the sides well

You can read small print but bump into doors, trip, or do not see people approaching. Typical of advanced glaucoma, retinitis pigmentosa and some brain injuries.

Helps: orientation and mobility training, a cane (often only at night) and good lighting.

{{icon:contrast}} Light bothers me or I see poor contrast

Light dazzles you (photophobia), things look washed out, or it is hard to tell a step from the floor. Happens with cataracts, corneal problems, albinism, retinal dystrophies and after some surgery.

Helps: selective filters, a peaked cap, dark mode on screens and more contrast at home.

{{icon:info}} Each eye sees differently, or it changes

This is “mixed vision”: one eye sees far but without detail and the other hardly at all, or you see well by day and very poorly at night. Others do not always understand.

Helps: a low vision assessment to make the most of each eye, and material to explain it to others.

By condition

  • AMD

    Age-related macular degeneration affects central vision. The wet form can be treated with injections if caught early; the dry form progresses slowly. If straight lines suddenly look wavy, go to A&E.

  • Glaucoma

    Damages the optic nerve and usually starts at the edges, unnoticed. Treatment (drops, laser, surgery or drainage valves) slows it down. Severe eye pain with a red eye is an emergency, even if you have had surgery.

  • Diabetic retinopathy and retinal vein occlusion

    Diabetes and high blood pressure can damage the retina's blood vessels: vision becomes blurred, patchy or lacking detail. Controlling them and attending check-ups is the best protection.

  • Retinitis pigmentosa

    Inherited. Often starts with poor night vision, then side vision narrows, like looking through a tube. A genetic test can confirm it.

  • High myopia

    Very high short-sightedness that may come with damage to the macula, macular holes, retinal detachment, glaucoma or cataract. Regular check-ups are very important.

  • Optic nerve conditions

    Inherited (such as Leber optic neuropathy), caused by a tumour pressing on the nerve (for example, a pituitary tumour), inflammation or poor blood supply. They usually affect central vision.

  • Cornea and transplants

    Infections, dystrophies or injuries of the cornea can cause blurred vision and strong discomfort from light. After a transplant, ask about your outlook and what to expect.

  • Cataract and intraocular lens

    A cataract clouds the lens and surgery usually works well. If the eye has other conditions, the improvement may be limited: ask beforehand.

There are other causes too, such as congenital cataracts, albinism or brain injury (cerebral visual impairment). Your ophthalmologist will explain your case.

Rare diseases

Many conditions that cause low vision are rare diseases: Leber hereditary optic neuropathy, Stargardt disease, Leber congenital amaurosis, choroideremia, achromatopsia, albinism or Usher syndrome, among others.

  • Having an uncommon condition does not mean nothing can be done: low vision aids and rehabilitation work just as well.
  • A genetic test can confirm the diagnosis, inform the family and open the door to clinical trials.
  • Patient associations for a specific condition are a good source of information and contact with other families.

What to ask at your appointment

Bring these questions written down, or ask someone to come with you:

  • What exactly is my condition called? Could you write it down?
  • Is there treatment? What can I expect over the coming months or years?
  • How often should I be checked? Which signs mean I should come back sooner?
  • Can you refer me for a low vision assessment and to social work?
  • Are there associations or clinical trials for my condition?
