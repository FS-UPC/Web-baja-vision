Supporting and guiding safely

Short, practical guides. Each one can be played with the Listen to this guide button, so you can learn it while doing other things.

The basics

  1. Ask before helping and how they would like to be helped.
  2. Say who you are when you arrive and tell them when you leave.
  3. Be specific: “on your right”, “two steps ahead”.
  4. Do not move things without saying so.
  5. Respect canes and guide dogs: do not touch or distract them.

Guiding outdoors: offering your arm

  1. Offer your arm

    Ask if they would like help. If yes, lightly touch the back of their hand with your arm so they can find it. Do not grab or push them.

  2. They hold just above your elbow

    Walk half a step ahead at a comfortable pace. That way they feel your movements before you make them.

  3. Describe what is around

    Mention changes in the ground, kerbs, obstacles at head height and people approaching.

  4. Sitting down

    Guide their hand to the back of the chair and let them sit down themselves.

Steps, doors and narrow spaces

  • {{icon:walk}} Steps and stairs

    Stop before the first step and say whether it goes up or down. Let them hold the handrail. Stay one step ahead and say when you reach the end.

  • {{icon:home}} Doors

    Say whether the door opens towards you or away, and on which side. The person being guided can hold it as they go through.

  • {{icon:arrow}} Narrow spaces

    Move your arm behind your back: this is the signal for them to step directly behind you. Return to normal afterwards.

  • {{icon:alert}} Public transport

    Point out the door, the step and the handrail. Say whether there are free seats and guide their hand to the back of one.

Communicating well

  • {{icon:chat}} Arriving and leaving

    Say your name when you arrive (“Hi, it's Martha”) and tell them when you leave, so they are not left talking to no one.

  • {{icon:arrow}} Precise directions

    Use references like “on your left” or clock positions: “your glass is at three o'clock”. Avoid “here” and “there”.

  • {{icon:eye}} Describe without worry

    It is fine to say “look” or “see”. Describe what is happening: who has arrived, what is on the plate, what a sign says.

  • {{icon:ear}} If they also have hearing loss

    Face them, with light on your face, without shouting and somewhere quiet. Check they have understood.

Helping without overprotecting

Wanting to protect is natural, but doing everything for the other person takes away their confidence and independence.

  • Let them do things at their own pace, even if it takes longer.
  • Help adapt the task (more light, markers, order) instead of doing it for them.
  • Do not make decisions for them: ask.
  • Encourage rehabilitation and learning technology: it will give them independence.

Caring for yourself to care for others

Sight loss is a process of adjustment for the whole family. There may be times of sadness, anger or denial. Listening and patience help a lot.

You matter too. If you feel overwhelmed, seek support: family groups, associations or psychological services. See where to find psychological support.
