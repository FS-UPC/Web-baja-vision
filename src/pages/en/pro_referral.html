Referral map

What to do depending on who detects the sight loss. In every case, give the patient a resource pack with a QR code.

{{icon:eye}} Ophthalmology

When: diagnosis of a condition that leaves sight loss that cannot be fully corrected.

  1. Explain the diagnosis and outlook in plain language (see communicating the diagnosis).
  2. Refer for a low vision assessment (specialist optometry, for example the CUV-UPC), without waiting for severe cases.
  3. Refer to social work for the disability certificate and benefits.
  4. Offer psychological support from the start.
  5. Inform about associations and, if criteria are met, ONCE.

{{icon:heart}} Primary care (CAP)

When: a patient reports seeing less, tripping, having stopped reading, or has a risk condition (diabetes, hypertension, glaucoma).

  1. Rule out urgent signs: if present, same-day urgent referral to ophthalmology.
  2. If not urgent, refer to ophthalmology and manage risk factors.
  3. If already diagnosed, refer to low vision services, social work and mental health if needed.
  4. Assess fall risk and isolation, especially in older people.

{{icon:search}} Optometry

When: visual acuity or field does not improve with the best correction.

  1. If there is no diagnosis, refer to ophthalmology.
  2. Carry out or refer for a functional low vision assessment.
  3. Coordinate training with rehabilitation and inform about resources.

{{icon:users}} Social work

When: anyone whose sight loss affects daily life.

  1. Advise on the disability certificate, long-term care and grants for assistive products.
  2. Connect with associations, peer support groups and community resources.
  3. Identify isolation or vulnerability.

Communicating the diagnosis

Participants remember for years how their diagnosis was given. Phrases like “nothing more can be done” or “with a retina like that, what do you expect?” leave patients with nowhere to go. Suggested good practice:

  1. Space and time: somewhere quiet, unhurried, offering to have a companion present.
  2. Find out what they know and want to know before explaining.
  3. Plain language and information in writing or an accessible format.
  4. Realism without hopelessness: explain what cannot be recovered and, above all, what can be done.
  5. A concrete next step: refer to professionals with more time and training (low vision optometry, social work, psychology) and hand over resources.
  6. Review at a later visit what they have understood and how they are.

Criteria for low vision referral

  • {{icon:eye}} Visual criteria

    • Visual acuity below 0.3 (6/18) in the better eye with best correction.
    • Significant visual field loss or central scotomas.
    • Reduced contrast sensitivity or disabling glare.

  • {{icon:person}} Functional criteria

    • Difficulty reading, writing, getting around or with daily living despite best correction.
    • Loss of independence or giving up meaningful activities.
    • Vision-related fall risk.

  • {{icon:heart}} Wellbeing criteria

    • Anxiety, low mood or isolation related to sight loss.
    • Need for guidance on resources, rights or benefits.
    • Possible Charles Bonnet syndrome.

Referral to vision rehabilitation should not be reserved for the most severe cases: early intervention eases adjustment.

{{icon:alert}}

Primary care: urgent signs

Refer to ophthalmology the same day if there is:

  • sudden vision loss in one or both eyes;
  • new-onset metamorphopsia (distorted lines);
  • photopsia, many new floaters or a curtain-like shadow;
  • severe eye pain with a red eye, headache, nausea or haloes (suspected acute intraocular pressure rise), including in patients who have had glaucoma surgery.

An acute pressure crisis cannot wait three months just because “it isn't cancer”: optic nerve damage is irreversible.
